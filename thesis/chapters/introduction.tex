\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

Large language models (LLMs) have become globally deployed systems, serving users across languages, cultures, and applications. Ensuring that these models behave safely — refusing harmful requests regardless of the language in which they are posed — is a fundamental challenge in AI safety. Contemporary safety alignment techniques, most notably Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)~\citep{ouyang2022training}, train models to refuse harmful requests through human preference data. However, this training process is heavily skewed toward English: the majority of annotated preference data, safety red-teaming, and evaluation benchmarks are developed in English~\citep{krasnodkebska2026safety, wang2024all}.

The consequence is a systematic disparity in safety coverage across languages. Empirical studies have consistently shown that safety-aligned LLMs exhibit substantially weaker refusal behavior when prompted in non-English languages, and that this gap widens as a function of how underrepresented the language is in training data~\citep{deng2024multilingual, shen2024language}. Most critically, Yong et al.~\citeyearpar{yong2023low} demonstrated that simply translating a harmful prompt into a low-resource language such as Zulu or Hmong was sufficient to bypass GPT-4's safety filters — a finding with significant safety implications for globally deployed systems.

\section{Problem}
\label{sec:problem}

Despite substantial empirical documentation of the multilingual safety gap, the underlying \textit{mechanism} of multilingual jailbreak vulnerability remains poorly understood. Two competing explanations can be proposed:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Suppression hypothesis:} Jailbreaking in any language works by suppressing an existing refusal mechanism. For high-resource languages, this mechanism is well-established and the jailbreak vector $\mathbf{j}_l$ should be anti-parallel to the refusal direction $\mathbf{r}_l$ (i.e., jailbreaking = suppressing refusal). This would predict that LRL jailbreak is mechanistically identical to HRL jailbreak, just easier due to a weaker refusal signal.

    \item \textbf{Absence hypothesis:} For low-resource languages, no meaningful refusal direction was established during safety training. Jailbreak success in these languages does not reflect the suppression of an existing mechanism but rather the model's default compliance in the absence of alignment. This predicts that $\mathbf{j}_l$ will be geometrically orthogonal to $\mathbf{r}_l$ in LRL settings.
\end{enumerate}

This distinction is not merely theoretical. If the absence hypothesis is correct, defense strategies that operate by ``restoring'' refusal (e.g., steering toward $-\mathbf{j}_l$) would need to \textit{inject} alignment rather than \textit{reinstate} it — a qualitatively different task, and one that existing inference-time methods have not been designed or evaluated for.

The problem this thesis addresses is therefore twofold: (1) determining which mechanism explains LRL vulnerability, and (2) testing whether cross-lingual transfer of jailbreak vectors enables both attacks and defenses that work across the HRL–LRL boundary.

\section{Research Question and Hypotheses}
\label{sec:rq}

The overarching research question is:

\begin{quote}
\textit{Do jailbreak vectors in multilingual LLMs exhibit cross-lingual geometric structure, and can this structure be exploited for both cross-lingual attack transfer and inference-time defense in low-resource languages?}
\end{quote}

This is operationalised through three testable hypotheses derived from the representation engineering literature~\citep{arditi2024refusal, zou2023representation} and the multilingual safety findings of Wang et al.~\citeyearpar{wang2026refusal}:

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{H1 (Geometry):}
\textit{For high-resource languages, the jailbreak vector $\mathbf{j}_l$ is anti-parallel to the refusal direction $\mathbf{r}_l$ ($\cos(\hat{\mathbf{j}}_l, \hat{\mathbf{r}}_l) \approx -1$). For low-resource languages, $\mathbf{j}_l$ is orthogonal to $\mathbf{r}_l$ ($\cos(\hat{\mathbf{j}}_l, \hat{\mathbf{r}}_l) \approx 0$), reflecting the absence of an established refusal mechanism.}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{H2 (Attack Transfer):}
\textit{Injecting a source language's jailbreak vector $\hat{\mathbf{j}}_\mathrm{src}$ into target-language activations at inference time increases bypass rates in the target language, including across the HRL–LRL boundary.}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{H3 (Defense Transfer):}
\textit{Subtracting a source language's jailbreak vector $\hat{\mathbf{j}}_\mathrm{src}$ from target-language activations at inference time reduces compliance rates, providing an effective inference-time defense even for LRL languages where same-language defense is unavailable.}

\section{Delimitations}
\label{sec:delimitations}

The scope of this thesis is bounded by the following delimitations:

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Models:} We evaluate three open-weight instruction-tuned models (Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, Gemma-2-9B-IT). Closed-source models such as GPT-4 are excluded, as activation-level interventions require access to internal representations.

    \item \textbf{Languages:} We study 16 languages — 13 high-resource and 3 low-resource (Yoruba, Swahili, Amharic). The HRL/LRL classification is based on estimated safety training coverage, not a formal linguistic resource metric.

    \item \textbf{Attack surface:} We focus on direct harmful instruction following (using the PolyRefuse benchmark~\citep{wang2026refusal}). Indirect jailbreak methods such as role-playing prompts or adversarial suffixes~\citep{zou2023universal} are out of scope.

    \item \textbf{Intervention type:} All experiments use inference-time activation steering. We do not study fine-tuning-based attacks or defenses.

    \item \textbf{Evaluation:} Safety classification is performed using WildGuard~\citep{han2024wildguard}. The binary refusal/compliance label is used as the primary metric; toxicity scoring and fine-grained harm categorisation are not considered.
\end{itemize}

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}

This thesis makes the following contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Jailbreak vector extraction without harmless data.} We introduce a method for extracting a per-language jailbreak vector $\mathbf{j}_l$ using only baseline harmful inference and automatic safety labelling. Unlike the refusal direction $\mathbf{r}_l$, this requires no harmless corpora.

    \item \textbf{Geometric characterisation of HRL vs.\ LRL vulnerability.} We provide empirical geometric evidence to distinguish between suppression-based jailbreak (HRL) and alignment-absent compliance (LRL).

    \item \textbf{Cross-lingual jailbreak attack transfer.} We demonstrate that jailbreak vectors transfer across languages, enabling source vectors to increase bypass rates in target languages.

    \item \textbf{Cross-lingual inference-time defense.} We show that subtracting a high-resource jailbreak vector from low-resource activations reduces harmful compliance — a training-free alignment intervention for data-sparse language settings.

    \item \textbf{Extended multilingual dataset.} We extend PolyRefuse~\citep{wang2026refusal} to 16 languages by adding Swahili and Amharic.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Thesis Structure}
\label{sec:structure}

This thesis follows the Testing a Hypothesis structure. \textbf{Part II} (Chapter~\ref{chap:background}) provides an extended background covering safety alignment, jailbreak attacks, multilingual safety, and representation engineering. \textbf{Part III} (Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology}) presents the methodology, including vector extraction, experimental design, and justification of research strategies. \textbf{Part IV} (Chapter~\ref{chap:results}) reports results for H1, H2, and H3. \textbf{Part V} (Chapter~\ref{chap:discussion}) discusses findings, limitations, and directions for future work.
