\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

Safety-aligned large language models (LLMs) are designed to refuse harmful requests. However, this safety behavior is not equally distributed across languages. Empirical studies have shown that models which reliably refuse harmful English prompts often comply with the same prompts when translated into low-resource languages~\citep{yong2023low, deng2024multilingual}. This phenomenon is known as the \textit{multilingual jailbreak vulnerability}.

The cause of this vulnerability is well understood at a high level: safety alignment relies on human feedback data, and such data is overwhelmingly English-centric~\citep{ouyang2022training, krasnodkebska2026safety}. As a result, models develop strong refusal behaviors in English and high-resource languages, but weak or absent refusal behaviors in low-resource languages. Yong et al.~\citeyearpar{yong2023low} showed that translating harmful prompts into Zulu or Hmong was sufficient to bypass GPT-4's safety filters. Deng et al.~\citeyearpar{deng2024multilingual} confirmed this pattern across 54 languages, showing that bypass rates increase consistently as language resource level decreases.

This thesis contributes to the intersection of natural language processing and AI safety, specifically to the area of multilingual safety in large language models. It examines the multilingual jailbreak vulnerability from a representation engineering perspective, studying the internal activation geometry that underlies jailbreak behavior across languages.

\section{Problem}
\label{sec:problem}

What remains less understood is the internal mechanism behind the multilingual jailbreak vulnerability. Two explanations have been proposed. In high-resource languages (HRL), jailbreaking may work by actively suppressing the model's established refusal behavior. In low-resource languages (LRL), the model may never have developed a refusal behavior to begin with. These two cases call for different solutions, yet existing research has not distinguished between them at the representation level.

Recent work has shown that refusal behavior can be characterised as a linear direction $\mathbf{r}_l$ in the model's activation space~\citep{arditi2024refusal}, and that this direction is geometrically consistent across safety-aligned high-resource languages~\citep{wang2026refusal}. However, three problems remain unaddressed.

\textbf{First}, it is not known whether jailbreak behavior can analogously be characterised as a direction $\mathbf{j}_l$, or whether the geometric relationship between $\mathbf{j}_l$ and $\mathbf{r}_l$ differs between HRL and LRL languages. If jailbreak behavior reflects suppression of refusal, one possible expectation is that $\mathbf{j}_l$ and $\mathbf{r}_l$ may be anti-parallel in HRL languages. For LRL languages, where refusal alignment may be absent, the relationship between these directions may be qualitatively different.

\textbf{Second}, it is not known whether jailbreak vectors transfer across languages. If the geometric structure of $\mathbf{j}_l$ is shared, injecting a source-language jailbreak vector into a target language may affect bypass rates. This has not been empirically tested.

\textbf{Third}, no inference-time defense exists for low-resource languages that lack sufficient data for safety fine-tuning. Whether subtracting a high-resource jailbreak vector from low-resource activations can provide a training-free alignment signal remains open.

These problems are both theoretical and practical in nature. Theoretically, they concern the internal geometry of safety representations in multilingual models. Practically, answering them could inform the design of inference-time safety interventions for languages where fine-tuning-based approaches are infeasible.

\section{Research Questions}
\label{sec:rq}

This thesis addresses three research questions, each corresponding to one of the problems identified above.

\begin{description}
    \item[\textbf{RQ1}] Is the jailbreak vector $\mathbf{j}_l$ geometrically related to the refusal direction $\mathbf{r}_l$, and does this relationship differ between high-resource and low-resource languages?

    \item[\textbf{RQ2}] Can a source-language jailbreak vector $\hat{\mathbf{j}}_\mathrm{src}$ be injected into target-language activations at inference time to increase bypass rates?

    \item[\textbf{RQ3}] Can subtracting a source-language jailbreak vector from target-language activations reduce compliance rates, including in low-resource languages where same-language defense is not available?
\end{description}

Answering RQ1 clarifies the mechanism of multilingual jailbreak vulnerability. Answering RQ2 and RQ3 tests whether this mechanism can be exploited and countered across linguistic boundaries, with practical implications for low-resource language safety.

\subsection*{Research Expectations}

Based on prior findings in representation engineering~\citep{arditi2024refusal, wang2026refusal}, the following outcomes are anticipated, though not assumed.

For RQ1, HRL languages may exhibit stronger anti-parallel alignment between $\mathbf{j}_l$ and $\mathbf{r}_l$ than LRL languages. LRL languages may show weaker or qualitatively different geometric relationships, reflecting an absence of safety alignment rather than its suppression.

For RQ2, if jailbreak vectors capture language-independent safety representations, we expect partial cross-lingual transfer, with bypass rates increasing in the target language when a source jailbreak vector is injected. Transfer may be more consistent in HRL-to-HRL settings than across the HRL-LRL boundary.

For RQ3, if the transferred vectors encode safety-relevant geometry, subtracting them may reduce harmful compliance in the target language. This effect may be weaker for LRL targets, where the underlying mechanism of compliance may differ from that in aligned languages.

\section{Delimitations}
\label{sec:delimitations}

The scope of this thesis is bounded by the following delimitations.

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Models.} Three open-weight instruction-tuned models are evaluated: Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, and Gemma-2-9B-IT. Closed-source models are excluded because activation-level interventions require access to internal representations.

    \item \textbf{Languages.} Sixteen languages are studied: 13 high-resource and 3 low-resource (Yoruba, Swahili, Amharic). The HRL/LRL classification reflects estimated safety training coverage, not a formal linguistic resource metric.

    \item \textbf{Attack type.} Only direct harmful instruction following is studied, using the PolyRefuse benchmark~\citep{wang2026refusal}. Prompt-engineering attacks such as role-playing or adversarial suffixes~\citep{zou2023universal} are outside the scope of this thesis.

    \item \textbf{Intervention type.} All experiments use inference-time activation steering. Fine-tuning-based attacks and defenses are not studied.

    \item \textbf{Evaluation.} Safety classification uses WildGuard~\citep{han2024wildguard}. The binary refusal label is the primary metric.
\end{itemize}

\section{Thesis Structure}
\label{sec:structure}

This thesis follows an empirical research design centred on three research questions. \textbf{Chapter~\ref{chap:background}} provides an extended background on multilingual jailbreak, representation engineering, and related work. \textbf{Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology}} describes the research strategy, vector extraction method, and the empirical investigation for each research question. \textbf{Chapter~\ref{chap:results}} reports the results addressing RQ1, RQ2, and RQ3. \textbf{Chapter~\ref{chap:discussion}} reflects on the findings, discusses limitations, and suggests directions for future work.
