\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

Safety-aligned large language models (LLMs) are designed to refuse harmful requests. However, this safety behavior is not equally distributed across languages. Empirical studies have shown that models which reliably refuse harmful English prompts often comply with the same prompts when translated into low-resource languages~\citep{yong2023low, deng2024multilingual}. This phenomenon is known as the \textit{multilingual jailbreak vulnerability}.

The cause of this vulnerability is well understood at a high level: safety alignment relies on human feedback data, and such data is overwhelmingly English-centric~\citep{ouyang2022training, krasnodkebska2026safety}. As a result, models develop strong refusal behaviors in English and high-resource languages, but weak or absent refusal behaviors in low-resource languages. Yong et al.~\citeyearpar{yong2023low} showed that translating harmful prompts into Zulu or Hmong was sufficient to bypass GPT-4's safety filters. Deng et al.~\citeyearpar{deng2024multilingual} confirmed this pattern across 54 languages, showing that bypass rates increase consistently as language resource level decreases.

What remains less understood is the internal mechanism behind this vulnerability. Two different explanations are possible. In high-resource languages, jailbreaking may work by actively suppressing the model's established refusal behavior. In low-resource languages, the model may simply never have developed a refusal behavior in those languages. These two cases call for different solutions, yet existing research has not distinguished between them at the representation level.

\section{Problem}
\label{sec:problem}

Recent work on representation engineering has shown that refusal behavior in LLMs can be characterised as a linear direction in the model's internal activation space~\citep{arditi2024refusal}. This \textit{refusal direction} $\mathbf{r}_l$ can be extracted using the Difference-in-Means method and used to steer model behavior at inference time. Wang et al.~\citeyearpar{wang2026refusal} showed that this direction is geometrically consistent across safety-aligned languages, suggesting a shared internal mechanism for refusal across high-resource languages.

However, three problems remain unaddressed in the existing literature.

\textbf{First}, it is not known whether jailbreak behavior can be analogously characterised as a direction $\mathbf{j}_l$ in activation space, and whether the relationship between $\mathbf{j}_l$ and $\mathbf{r}_l$ differs between high-resource and low-resource languages. If jailbreaking works by suppressing refusal, then $\mathbf{j}_l$ should be anti-parallel to $\mathbf{r}_l$. If low-resource languages lack refusal alignment entirely, then $\mathbf{j}_l$ should be geometrically unrelated to $\mathbf{r}_l$.

\textbf{Second}, it is not known whether jailbreak vectors transfer across languages. If the geometric structure of $\mathbf{j}_l$ is shared across languages, then injecting a source-language jailbreak vector into a target language should increase bypass rates. This has not been tested.

\textbf{Third}, no inference-time defense exists for low-resource languages that lack sufficient training data for safety fine-tuning. If a high-resource jailbreak vector can be subtracted from low-resource activations to restore refusal, this would provide a training-free alignment method for data-scarce language settings.

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Research Questions}
\label{sec:rq}

This thesis addresses three research questions.

\begin{description}
    \item[\textbf{RQ1}] Is the jailbreak vector $\mathbf{j}_l$ geometrically related to the refusal direction $\mathbf{r}_l$, and does this relationship differ between high-resource and low-resource languages?

    \item[\textbf{RQ2}] Can a source-language jailbreak vector $\hat{\mathbf{j}}_\mathrm{src}$ be injected into target-language activations at inference time to increase bypass rates?

    \item[\textbf{RQ3}] Can subtracting a source-language jailbreak vector from target-language activations reduce compliance rates, including in low-resource languages where same-language defense is not available?
\end{description}

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Expected Observations}
\label{sec:expected}

Based on the research questions above and prior findings in representation engineering~\citep{arditi2024refusal, wang2026refusal}, the following observations are anticipated.

For RQ1, HRL languages may exhibit stronger anti-parallel alignment between $\mathbf{j}_l$ and $\mathbf{r}_l$ than LRL languages. LRL languages may show weaker or qualitatively different geometric relationships, reflecting an absence of safety alignment rather than its suppression.

For RQ2, if jailbreak vectors capture language-independent safety representations, we expect partial cross-lingual transfer, with bypass rates increasing in the target language when a source jailbreak vector is injected. Transfer may be more consistent in HRL-to-HRL settings than across the HRL-LRL boundary.

For RQ3, if the transferred vectors encode safety-relevant geometry, subtracting them may reduce harmful compliance in the target language. This effect may be weaker for LRL targets, where the underlying mechanism of compliance differs from that in aligned languages.

\section{Delimitations}
\label{sec:delimitations}

The scope of this thesis is bounded by the following delimitations.

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Models.} Three open-weight instruction-tuned models are evaluated: Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, and Gemma-2-9B-IT. Closed-source models are excluded because activation-level interventions require access to internal representations.

    \item \textbf{Languages.} Sixteen languages are studied: 13 high-resource and 3 low-resource (Yoruba, Swahili, Amharic). The HRL/LRL classification reflects estimated safety training coverage, not a formal linguistic resource metric.

    \item \textbf{Attack type.} Only direct harmful instruction following is studied, using the PolyRefuse benchmark~\citep{wang2026refusal}. Prompt-engineering attacks such as role-playing or adversarial suffixes~\citep{zou2023universal} are outside the scope of this thesis.

    \item \textbf{Intervention type.} All experiments use inference-time activation steering. Fine-tuning-based attacks and defenses are not studied.

    \item \textbf{Evaluation.} Safety classification uses WildGuard~\citep{han2024wildguard}. The binary refusal label is the primary metric.
\end{itemize}

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}

This thesis makes the following contributions.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item A method for extracting a per-language jailbreak vector $\mathbf{j}_l$ from baseline inference without harmless training data, applicable to language settings where such data is unavailable.

    \item Geometric evidence distinguishing two mechanistically different causes of multilingual jailbreak vulnerability: refusal suppression in high-resource languages versus alignment absence in low-resource languages.

    \item Empirical results showing that jailbreak vectors transfer across languages, enabling source-language vectors to increase bypass rates in target languages.

    \item An inference-time cross-lingual defense that reduces harmful compliance in low-resource languages by subtracting a high-resource jailbreak vector, requiring no additional training data.

    \item An extended version of the PolyRefuse dataset~\citep{wang2026refusal} covering 16 languages, including Swahili and Amharic as new low-resource additions.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Thesis Structure}
\label{sec:structure}

This thesis follows the Testing a Hypothesis structure. \textbf{Part II} (Chapter~\ref{chap:background}) provides an extended background on multilingual jailbreak, representation engineering, and related work. \textbf{Part III} (Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology}) describes the methodology, including vector extraction, experimental design, and research strategy justification. \textbf{Part IV} (Chapter~\ref{chap:results}) reports results for H1, H2, and H3. \textbf{Part V} (Chapter~\ref{chap:discussion}) discusses findings, limitations, and directions for future work.
